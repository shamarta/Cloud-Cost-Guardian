# Customer Discovery Interview Kit

**Status:** Deferred while the founder learns cloud engineering and builds an educational prototype. No customer validation is implied.

Use this kit to test the customer and problem hypothesis in [market-validation.md](./market-validation.md). The goal is to learn how people handle real cost surprises, not to sell a proposed solution.

## Who to contact

Prioritize founders, CTOs, platform/DevOps engineers, or engineering leads at AWS-first AI startups with small engineering teams and no dedicated FinOps role. A contact is a fit only if they have first-hand experience with cloud or AI infrastructure costs.

Aim for 8–10 interviews. Do not treat friends' encouragement, likes, or survey votes as evidence of willingness to pay.

## Finding candidates on LinkedIn

Search for people using combinations of these role and company phrases:

- `Founder` or `CTO` with `AI startup` and `AWS`
- `Platform Engineer` or `DevOps Engineer` with `AI` and `AWS`
- `Infrastructure Engineer` at a small AI/SaaS startup

Use LinkedIn's location and company filters if they help narrow results, but do not assume a title proves the person owns cloud costs. Review the person's public profile and company information, then ask directly whether they have first-hand experience with the cost workflow before scheduling.

Start by identifying 10–15 plausible people, then send a small number of personalized connection requests each day. Mention one relevant public detail about their role or company; do not send a mass-pitch. Track invitations, replies, fit, interview date, and follow-up without storing sensitive personal details. If they do not respond, one polite follow-up is enough.

## Outreach message

Use English for international contacts and adapt the bracketed details. Keep the invitation focused on their experience, not on selling a product.

### LinkedIn connection request

> Hi [Name] — I’m researching how small AI startups manage AWS and AI infrastructure costs. I’m not pitching a product; I’m trying to learn from teams’ real experiences. Since you work on [relevant role/company detail], would you be open to a short 20-minute conversation about how your team handles unexpected cost changes?

### Follow-up after they accept

> Thanks for connecting. If you have time, I’d appreciate a 20-minute conversation about the last time your cloud or AI infrastructure costs were higher than expected and how your team investigated it. I’m doing early research, not selling anything, and won’t need confidential information or account access. Would [option 1] or [option 2] work for you?

## Suggested 20-minute interview

### Opening

> Thanks for taking the time. I’m researching how small teams manage AWS and AI infrastructure costs. I don’t have a product to sell, and I’m not looking for validation of an idea; I want to understand your actual experience. Please don’t share confidential numbers or information.

### Questions

Ask about actual past behavior. Avoid pitching features or asking leading questions like “Would you use an AI dashboard?”

1. What is your role, and how are you involved in cloud or AI infrastructure costs?
2. Tell me about the most recent time a cloud or AI bill was higher than expected.
3. How did the team discover the increase and investigate its cause?
4. How long did it take, and who was involved?
5. What tools, alerts, dashboards, or spreadsheets did you use?
6. What was difficult, missing, or unreliable in that process?
7. What impact did the increase or investigation have on the team?
8. How often has a similar situation happened?
9. Have you tried or paid for anything to solve this? What happened?
10. Who would decide whether to adopt and pay for a solution?
11. What would need to be true for your team to evaluate a different approach?
12. Would you be open to a follow-up about a read-only pilot if the problem is a fit?

### Close

Ask permission before taking notes that include a person's name or company. Do not request cloud credentials, account access, unredacted billing exports, or other sensitive material. If a data example would help, ask whether they can share a redacted and approved sample later.

## Evidence review

After each interview, record the strongest evidence and update the hypothesis. Do not count a feature request as proof that the underlying problem is frequent, costly, or budgeted.

Proceed to a narrow PRD only when the decision gate in [market-validation.md](./market-validation.md) is met: at least three independent accounts of the same recurring problem and workaround, two concrete follow-up commitments, and credible willingness to pay or a paid-pilot evaluation from at least one potential customer.
